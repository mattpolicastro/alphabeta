Bayesian analysis · Beta–Binomial

How likely is the treatment better?

The same conversion test read the Bayesian way: the probability each variant beats the control, what you stand to lose by choosing it, and a credible interval on the lift. Computed exactly by integrating the posterior — no simulation, so the same inputs always give the same digits.

Arms

Per arm: unique visitors and how many of them converted. The first arm is the control.

Beta(α, β) on every arm's rate. Beta(1, 1) is flat — the reference tool's choice, and the default. Beta(α, β) reads as α prior conversions among α + β prior visitors, so Beta(2, 20) says "about 9%, from 22 visitors' worth of belief".

% lift

A relative lift smaller than this is not worth acting on. 0 asks only "is it better at all?"

Mass in the interval on lift, and the bar a probability must clear for the one-line read.

Numbers are written in their shortest exact decimal form (0.95, not 0.950); lists use a literal comma. Model: independent Beta–Binomial per arm, posterior Beta(a + conversions, b + visitors − conversions). Every quantity is a one-dimensional integral over the control's posterior density, evaluated by composite Gauss–Legendre quadrature (96 panels × 12 points over the 10⁻¹⁵-quantile range of the density) with the regularized incomplete beta function for the CDF: P(lift > t) = ∫ f_c(x) · [1 − F_t((1 + t) x)] dx; expected loss E[max(0, p_c − p_t)] = ∫ f_c(x) · [x F_t(x) − μ_t F_{t+}(x)] dx; P(best) = ∫ f_i(x) · Π F_j(x) dx. The equal-tailed credible interval on lift inverts P(lift > t) by bisection. No sampling and no seed anywhere. The reference tool on forwarddigital.org (Merritt Aho) draws 100 000 rbeta samples with the same Beta(1, 1) prior and reports the same three probabilities; its numbers land within its own Monte Carlo noise of these (±0.5 points at 100 000 draws), which is why it rounds to whole percents. It omits expected loss on purpose; we include it, pairwise against the control.
